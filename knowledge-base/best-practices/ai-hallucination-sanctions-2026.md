# AI Hallucination Sanctions — 2026 Landscape

## Overview

By late April 2026, U.S. federal and state courts have moved decisively past the "novelty" phase of AI-citation sanctions and into a structured enforcement posture. Q1 2026 alone produced more than $145,000 in sanctions against attorneys for filings that contained AI-fabricated citations or quotations. Two additional inflection points landed in March–April 2026: the first federal *circuit court* sanctions, and the first public Big Law hallucination incident. The pattern is consistent across jurisdictions: the courts are not punishing the use of AI. They are punishing the failure to verify AI output against primary authority before filing, and — increasingly — they are punishing firms that had written policies on paper but no institutional checkpoint that caught this filing.

This knowledge-base entry summarizes the 2026 landscape as a design reference for the skills in this repo — particularly the `ai-citation-verifier` skill (drafter-side sweep), the `pre-filing-independent-review` skill (institutional second-verifier), and any skill that produces text that will be filed, served, or sent externally.

## Anchor Cases and Benchmarks

**District of Oregon — *Couvrette v. Wisnovsky* — April 4, 2026 — the *Brigandi* matter.** U.S. Magistrate Judge Mark Clarke entered an opinion imposing approximately $96,000 in direct sanctions against a pro bono attorney, with total monetary penalties exceeding $110,000 once opposing counsel's fees were added. Three filings contained 23 fabricated citations and 8 fabricated quotations in an intra-family dispute over an Oregon vineyard. The court relied on Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11 and Oregon RPC 3.3 (duty of candor to the tribunal), struck the errant briefs without leave to refile, and, on evidence of client participation in the pattern, dismissed the plaintiff's claims with prejudice. The opinion is notable for its explicit cover-up framing — "Rather than a correction, [counsel] attempted a cover-up. He failed at both" — and for its treatment of post-discovery concealment as a multiplier on the underlying citation-fabrication conduct. The cover-up framing has since become a template the *Gutierrez* court (below) drew on.

**Oregon Court of Appeals — December 2025 tariff schedule.** The court established a per-infraction rate for AI hallucination misconduct: $500 per fabricated citation and $1,000 per fabricated quotation. This rate has been cited in 2026 sanctions opinions as a floor rather than a ceiling.

**Fifth Circuit — *Fletcher v. Experian Information Solutions* — Spring 2026.** The first federal *circuit court* sanction specifically implicating AI-assisted drafting. Counsel filed a reply brief on appeal containing 16 fabricated quotations and 5 additional misrepresentations of law or fact. On the show-cause response, counsel admitted using two commercial legal AI platforms to "help organize and structure" argument. The court imposed a $2,500 monetary sanction and framed the matter as a lawyering problem rather than a technology problem: AI use does not reduce a lawyer's professional duties, and the signer is responsible for every quotation, citation, and factual representation.

**Sixth Circuit — *Whiting v. City of Athens, Tenn.* — March 13, 2026.** Two Tennessee attorneys were sanctioned $15,000 each (aggregate $30,000) plus full reimbursement of appellees' attorney fees and double court costs for filings containing more than two dozen fabricated or misrepresented citations across three consolidated appeals. The court did not expressly find AI use but articulated what became the governing line across the 2026 docket: "no filing should contain citations, however generated, that a lawyer has not personally read and verified." The court invoked its inherent authority and Appellate Rule 38.

**Southern District of Ohio — Q1 2026 — Senior Judge Walter H. Rice — contempt finding; disciplinary referral.** Two Ohio-based attorneys filed AI-assisted briefs whose fabricated or misrepresented citations the court characterised as "the most egregious, inexplicable, and repeated violations of Rule 11(b)" Judge Rice had encountered in his tenure. The court imposed a collective monetary sanction of $7,500 and — going beyond the monetary-sanction register — found the attorneys in *contempt of court*, a remedy distinct from and stacking atop the monetary-sanction tier, and referred both attorneys to the Ohio Supreme Court's Office of Disciplinary Counsel for further disciplinary proceedings. The case is notable on three dimensions for the repo's skill-design framework. First, the contempt finding elevates the AI-hallucination enforcement register from Rule 11 monetary sanctions into the inherent-authority-and-contempt tier, which carries immediate enforcement consequences (potential incarceration, stay of proceedings, and restrictions on court access) distinct from the disciplinary-referral track. Second, the "repeated violations" framing tracks the pattern-of-conduct finding rather than a single errant brief: the court's characterization implies the citation-fabrication was not an isolated oversight but a recurrent feature of the filing record, reinforcing that multiple-filing citation-fabrication patterns draw qualitatively more severe responses than single-filing incidents. Third, the simultaneous imposition of three independent layers of consequence — monetary sanction, contempt finding, and disciplinary referral — in a single district-court proceeding, without requiring a prior first-offense record to trigger the bar-referral step, illustrates that the escalation-ladder model of the Eastern District of Pennsylvania's April 27 second-strike opinion is not the only path to a disciplinary referral: when a court views the conduct as sufficiently "egregious," all three consequences can land at once on an initial proceeding. The Ohio case accordingly reinforces the repo's pre-filing design principle that the institutional checkpoint must catch the pattern before it reaches the court, not merely the single errant filing.

**California Court of Appeal — *Noland v. Nazar*, B331918 (2d App. Dist.).** The first state appellate opinion to sanction an attorney for AI hallucinations. A $10,000 sanction was imposed and the opinion was published "as a warning." The court's framing — that hallucinated facts and law can be "circulated, believed, and become 'fact' and 'law' in some minds" — anticipates the adverse-authority and mischaracterization error categories that dominate the 2026 defect taxonomy.

**District of New Jersey — *Gutierrez v. Lorenzo Food Group* — April 2026 — Judge Evelyn Padin.** A wrongful-termination brief opposing a motion to dismiss contained citations attributed to the wrong cases, decade-stale "bad law" authorities, and quotations that did not match the cases they were attached to. The court issued a show-cause order. After months of investigation — conflicting affidavits, finger-pointing among the firm's lawyers, and an evidentiary hearing — Judge Padin concluded that no generative AI tool had been used at all. The errors traced to a paralegal who had been substantively drafting briefs and who, when instructed to swap out-of-circuit citations for Third Circuit cases, replaced the citations but left the original quotations in place. No attorney cite-checked the final brief or compared the new citations to the surviving quotations. The signing attorney's initial response — affidavits blaming a former associate — was found to be a cover-up, and the court was "deeply troubled" by it. The lead attorney was sanctioned (the monetary amount to be set after the fee certification), ordered to complete two CLE courses (one on legal ethics and one on AI in legal practice), and the opinion grounds the sanction in violations of ABA Model Rule 1.1 (competence), Rule 5.1(a) (supervisory responsibilities of partners and managing lawyers), and Rule 5.3(a) (responsibilities regarding nonlawyer assistance). *Gutierrez* is the cleanest 2026 illustration that the supervisory-failure pattern courts are punishing in AI-citation cases is the same pattern they punish when AI is *not* involved — when the firm's response to the show-cause order tries to make AI the cover story for an underlying supervision failure, the court treats the cover-up as an aggravator, not a defense.

**New York Supreme Court, Suffolk County — *Cassata v. Michael Macrina Architect, P.C.*, 2026 NY Slip Op 26014 — Justice Linda Kevins.** A house-collapse damages action in Westhampton Dunes. Defense counsel filed opposition papers containing fabricated case citations, fabricated quotations, and legal propositions unsupported by the cited authority. The court found a Rule 1.1 (competence) violation, plus failure of supervisory oversight under the New York analogues to ABA Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3, and held that "lawyers may not abdicate these core professional responsibilities to an AI platform and submit its output to the Court without meaningful review." Sanction: defense counsel and the supervising attorney each fined $1,000 payable to the Lawyer's Fund for Client Protection, and the firm itself ordered to pay $8,000 in plaintiff's attorney fees, costs, and expenses. *Cassata* establishes — at the state-trial-court level — that the firm itself, distinct from the signing attorney, is a sanctionable party when supervisory infrastructure is absent.

**Big Law incident — *In re Prince Global Holdings Ltd.* (Chapter 15, S.D.N.Y. Bankr.) — April 2026.** On April 18, 2026, a partner at a top-tier Wall Street firm filed a letter to the Chief Judge of the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New York apologizing for AI-generated errors in an April 9 emergency motion and in four accompanying documents (the verified petition, the joint administration motion, the scheduling motion, and two declarations). A Schedule A attached to the apology letter catalogued approximately 40 corrections: statutory misquotations of the Bankruptcy Code, mischaracterizations of authority, and at least one fabricated case. Opposing counsel — not the filing firm's internal review — surfaced the errors. The firm's own statement acknowledged that "notwithstanding its comprehensive policies and training requirements on AI use, the protocols were not followed, and the firm's ordinary citation-checking processes also failed to catch the invented authorities and other mistakes." As of the apology letter, the court had not yet ruled on sanctions. The incident is the cleanest 2026 illustration that written AI policies and "ordinary citation-checking processes" alone are not sufficient — a firm needs an institutional second-verifier checkpoint whose attestation does not depend on the drafting team's self-report.

**Nebraska Supreme Court — *In re Discipline of Lake* (Omaha attorney) — April 15–16, 2026 — first U.S. license suspension for AI-hallucination misconduct.** A solo-practitioner attorney filed a divorce appellate brief before the Nebraska Supreme Court. Of the brief's 63 cited authorities, 57 were defective on their face — including 20 outright hallucinations (citations to authorities that did not exist) and 3 entirely fabricated cases generated by an AI tool. At February 2026 oral argument, the justices questioned the attorney directly about the citation pattern and asked whether AI had been used; the attorney initially denied the AI provenance. He later — in a written response to a show-cause order — admitted that he had used a generative AI tool to draft the brief and characterized the initial denial as a "grave error of judgment." The Nebraska Supreme Court entered an order on April 15–16, 2026 indefinitely suspending the attorney from the practice of law in Nebraska "until further notice," referring the matter to the Counsel for Discipline for further proceedings, and grounded the order in failures of competence (Rule 1.1), candor (Rule 3.3), and the disciplinary obligation to be forthright with a tribunal in response to direct judicial inquiry. The opinion's framing of the underlying duty: AI "can be a benefit to the legal community, but it must be used with caution and humility," and the citation defects were "easily preventable" by routine verification on standard legal-research platforms. *Lake* is the first U.S. attorney-discipline action to suspend the practice license of an attorney whose underlying misconduct was driven by AI-fabricated citations, and it graduates the 2026 enforcement posture from monetary-sanction-and-CLE remedies into a license-impacting tier. The opinion is also the first published 2026 disciplinary action in which the *initial denial of AI use* in response to direct judicial inquiry is treated as an independent and aggravating duty-of-candor violation distinct from the underlying citation-fabrication conduct — the same cover-up-as-aggravator pattern *Couvrette / Brigandi* and *Gutierrez* established at the trial-court sanction level, now carried into the disciplinary tier. Worth contrasting with the Eastern District of Pennsylvania escalation ladder (immediately below): *Lake* shows that a single first-instance disciplinary referral can reach license suspension when the case combines a heavy citation-defect count with a duty-of-candor failure — the structured-escalation-ladder framework is not the only path to a license-impacting outcome.

**Alabama Supreme Court — *Ibach v. Stewart*, No. SC-2025-0106 (Ala. Apr. 24, 2026) — second 2026 state-supreme-court order; conditional filing-bar / co-signature remedy; *apology-with-AI* failure pattern.** A Mobile solo-practitioner appeared as counsel for the appellants in a family-trust appeal arising from Mobile Circuit Court. The opening and reply briefs cited "numerous invalid, inaccurate, and/or irrelevant" authorities that "appear to be artificial-intelligence ('AI') 'hallucinations,' i.e., fake authorities created by an AI system" — including a purported Nebraska Court of Appeals citation that traced to an unrelated Iowa criminal matter, and a quotation attributed to an Illinois trust opinion that traced to a withdrawn Indiana ruling. In a footnote in the reply brief, counsel apologized for the AI-hallucinated authorities and promised "the mistake will not recur" — and then, immediately after that declaration in the same footnote sequence, cited two further authorities that did not exist. Justice Greg Cook's opinion: "It is simply hard to imagine how this could occur absent, perhaps, using AI to craft the apology for having used AI." The court dismissed the appeal as "frivolous" because the briefing was "grossly deficient," ordered counsel to pay the appellees $17,200 in attorneys' fees and costs, ordered counsel to pay double the costs of the appeal payable to the court, referred the matter to the Alabama State Bar for disciplinary investigation, and entered a *conditional filing-bar*: counsel is prohibited from filing anything else in the Alabama Supreme Court unless another attorney in good standing with the Alabama State Bar signs the filing. *Hall* is significant on three independent dimensions. First, it is the second 2026 state-supreme-court order in nine days (after Nebraska's April 15–16 *Lake* order) to act directly against an attorney for AI-hallucination misconduct, confirming that a state-direct-disciplinary path to license-impacting consequences is now an established multi-jurisdiction pattern rather than a single-court anomaly. Second, it is the first published 2026 sanctions order to impose a *conditional filing-bar / co-signature remedy* — a forward-looking remedy that does not suspend the license outright but requires every future filing in the issuing court to be co-signed by an unrelated good-standing attorney, which operationalizes the supervisory-checkpoint failure the court identifies as the proximate cause and forces the institutional second-verifier into the workflow as a court-ordered condition rather than as a firm-internal policy. The remedy sits between *Cassata*'s firm-fine tier and *Lake*'s license-suspension tier as a third intermediate consequence on the disciplinary-tier ladder. Third, it is the first published 2026 sanctions order to identify the *apology-with-AI* failure pattern — a second-order pattern in which the post-error apology drafted as a corrective is itself drafted with the same generative tool that produced the underlying defect, repeating the citation-fabrication failure inside the apology that purports to remediate it. The apology-with-AI pattern is structurally distinct from the post-error cover-up of *Couvrette / Brigandi* and *Gutierrez* (failure mode #7) and from the denial-of-AI-on-direct-inquiry of *Lake* (failure mode #10): the cover-up shifts attribution to evade responsibility, the direct-inquiry denial misrepresents AI provenance to the tribunal, and the apology-with-AI recommits the same generative misconduct in the act of acknowledging it. The opinion is also notable for the court's framing of the underlying duty as the same one *Whiting*, *Fletcher*, and *Lake* articulate — that the signer is responsible for every citation in the brief regardless of source — and for surfacing a new defect-pattern variant the citation verifier should learn: *cross-jurisdictional citation jumbling*, in which an AI tool substitutes the citation but leaves the quoted text or the proposition attached to the wrong court, the wrong matter type, or a withdrawn or vacated opinion. The opinion was published 5 days before the prior monitor pass and was missed by the 2026-04-29 monitor scan; surfaced this cycle.

**District of Maine — *Hyde School* class action (Justice Law Collaborative) — May 5, 2026 — U.S. District Judge Stacey D. Neumann — first 2026 *candor-mitigates* anchor case.** A Massachusetts attorney representing the plaintiff class in a Hyde School (Bath, Maine) abuse-and-forced-labor class action filed an opposition to the defendant's October 2025 motion to dismiss that contained two improperly cited federal cases and an inaccurately quoted Maine human-trafficking statute, among other authority defects. Several months later — after the court extended a 60-day period to retain Maine-based local counsel — counsel disclosed in an April 28, 2026 filing that the original opposition's defective authorities had been produced using either ChatGPT or Claude and submitted without an independent cite-and-quote check. On the show-cause record, counsel accepted responsibility for the errors and expressed contrition. In her May 5, 2026 order, Judge Neumann declined to impose any monetary penalty and instead ordered: (a) completion of a continuing-legal-education course on the use of AI in legal practice; and (b) implementation of new firm-level guardrails to prevent recurrence. The order frames the underlying duty in the same terms as *Whiting*, *Fletcher*, *Cassata*, and *Lake* — "Although AI can be a useful aid in research and drafting, its use does not diminish an attorney's nondelegable duties of diligence, candor, and reasonable inquiry" — while explicitly identifying counsel's acceptance of responsibility and expressed remorse as the basis for declining to fine. *Hyde School / Guagenty* is the first published 2026 opinion in the federal-district sanctions docket in which the *mitigating* register — prompt candor, voluntary disclosure of AI provenance before judicial inquiry, acceptance of responsibility, and remediation commitments — is identified as the operative factor in selecting a non-monetary remedy where the underlying citation-defect pattern would have supported a Rule 11 monetary sanction. The opinion completes the candor-and-remediation framework that *Couvrette / Brigandi*, *Gutierrez*, *Lake*, and *Hall / Ibach* established on the aggravating side: the same body of evidence the court evaluates to apply a multiplier (cover-up, denial-on-direct-inquiry, apology-with-AI) is the body of evidence it evaluates to remit a monetary penalty when the disclosure pattern runs in the opposite direction. The cleanest operational read for the repo's pre-filing checkpoint and certification-checklist design: the AI-tool log entry, the chain-of-custody record, and the supervisory-review trail are not merely defensive — they are the predicate the signing attorney needs in hand on the day a defect is discovered to choose prompt disclosure over delay, and that choice is the difference between a non-monetary remedy and a published monetary sanction (with the *Lake* and *Hall / Ibach* paths both potentially in play once the disclosure-window has closed).

**Florida Supreme Court — *In re Amendments to Florida Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration*, No. SC2026-0673 (Fla. May 28, 2026) — first statewide rule-based AI certification regime.** The Florida Supreme Court amended Rule of General Practice and Judicial Administration 2.515(d)(2) to require that every signer of a court filing — attorney or self-represented litigant — represent that all legal authorities cited in the filing exist and are accurately cited. The amendment takes effect June 15, 2026 and applies to all state courts statewide. The court's framing is structural rather than incident-driven: the amendment was adopted "principally to create a statewide, uniform replacement for varied circuit court administrative orders imposing disclosure and certification requirements about the use of artificial intelligence in filings." The rule has four design features that distinguish it from the circuit-by-circuit administrative orders the repo previously tracked. (i) *Signature-as-certification rather than separate AI disclosure.* The amendment does not require a separate AI certification or AI disclosure form attached to the filing; instead, the signer's signature itself carries the representation that every cited authority exists and is accurately cited. The rule does not prohibit AI use, and does not require disclosure of which AI tool was used or at which stage of drafting — a deliberate narrowing relative to the Florida 11th and 17th Circuit administrative orders the repo tracked in `ai-disclosure-court-orders-2026.md`. (ii) *Express preemption of circuit and local AI-certification orders.* The amendment "obviates the need for circuit-level disclosure or certification requirements about the use of artificial intelligence" and accordingly bars Florida lower courts from imposing such requirements through local administrative orders, court policies, judicial practices and procedures, or other means. In one rule-amendment cycle the Florida Supreme Court collapses the Miami-Dade AO 26-04 (Jan. 15, 2026), Broward AO 2026-03-Gen (Jan. 26, 2026), Palm Beach AO 2.109-4/26 (Apr. 10, 2026), and any chambers-rule analogue into a single statewide signature-certification regime. (iii) *Enumerated sanction menu for failure.* The revised rule expressly authorizes lower courts — on their own motion or on a party's motion — to impose sanctions for filings containing legal authorities that do not exist or are inaccurately cited, with the available sanctions enumerated as reprimand, contempt, striking the filing, dismissal of proceedings, costs, attorneys' fees, and other appropriate remedies. The contempt remedy was previously reserved for *Lake*-class direct disciplinary referrals and *Rice* (S.D. Ohio) inherent-authority findings; the Florida rule makes it a statewide first-instance trial-court remedy. (iv) *Coverage of self-represented litigants on equal terms with attorneys.* The signature-as-certification rule reaches both attorneys and SRLs, continuing the Palm Beach County administrative-order trend rather than the federal-district chambers-rule split. *In re Amendments* is significant on three independent dimensions for this repo's design framework. First, it is the first state-supreme-court adoption of a *statewide rule-based* (rather than per-court order-based) AI-citation certification regime, and the first to expressly preempt the circuit-and-chambers-rule patchwork in favor of a uniform signature-certification — a structural shift the repo's `ai-disclosure-court-orders-2026.md` jurisdiction-index design must absorb. Second, the signature-as-certification design eliminates the certification-form-mismatch failure mode the prior 2026 record exposed (filings sanctioned not for substantive defects but for omitting the prescribed certification language) and replaces it with the underlying substantive duty: any cited authority that does not exist or is inaccurately cited triggers the sanction menu regardless of whether AI was used at all. Third, the contempt-as-first-instance-remedy escalates the trial-court enforcement register: a Florida circuit judge can now reach the contempt tier without traversing the federal-court escalation ladder, without a prior referral to the disciplinary track, and without the *Lake* state-supreme-court disciplinary path — the statewide rule supplies the predicate, and the inherent contempt power supplies the consequence. The clean operational read for the repo: the `ai-citation-verifier` certification checklist should add a Florida row that records "all cited authorities exist and are accurately cited" as the signature-attached representation (not a separate AI-disclosure form), and the `pre-filing-independent-review` four-category error sweep (citations / quotations / characterizations / statutory text) is the operational predicate for the signing attorney's representation. The Florida rule does not change the substantive verification duty; it crystallizes the duty into the signature itself and makes the consequence ladder available without the disciplinary-tribunal detour.

**Eastern District of Pennsylvania — second-strike sanction (Cherry Hill, NJ-based attorney) — April 27, 2026 — Judge Kai N. Scott.** The first published 2026 opinion to impose a *second-strike* AI-hallucination sanction on the same attorney. The signing attorney had previously been sanctioned $2,500 for AI-fabricated authorities; on the second offense, the court raised the monetary sanction to $5,000, ordered the attorney to complete additional continuing-legal-education courses specifically on artificial intelligence and legal ethics (and to file proof of any prior AI-and-ethics CLE he had already taken), and warned that a third instance of the same conduct would draw an immediate referral to the Pennsylvania Disciplinary Board (the attorney holds his license in Pennsylvania while practicing out of New Jersey). The brief at issue contained six bad citations across a single filing — five with errors and one to a case that did not exist. The attorney's show-cause response disclosed that he had drafted the brief using one commercial AI chatbot under time pressure and "verified" the citations by feeding them to a second AI chatbot — the *AI-verifying-AI* failure pattern, in which the verification step is delegated to a system that has the same generative pathology as the drafter. The opinion is also memorable for the court's framing of the underlying duty: "law is fundamentally a human enterprise," and "if you do [forget your duties to your clients, courts, or society], it will be your name and license on the line," not the chatbot's. The case is the cleanest 2026 illustration of a structured judicial-enforcement *escalation ladder* applied to a single attorney across two filings: monetary sanction → enhanced monetary sanction plus mandatory AI-and-ethics CLE → conditional bar-referral threat. It is also the first published 2026 opinion to identify "AI used to verify AI" as the proximate cause of the verification failure rather than a downstream consequence of inattention.

**Q1 2026 aggregate.** Commentary across Law.com, NPR, and state-bar publications tallies at least $145,000 in AI-hallucination sanctions in the first three months of 2026. With the 5th and 6th Circuit sanctions and the California state-appellate opinion layered on, the enforcement posture now spans three federal circuits, at least one state appellate court, and multiple district courts. The tally does not include reputational and disciplinary costs, case-dispositive sanctions (strike-with-prejudice orders), fee-shifting awards, or the not-yet-ruled April 2026 Big Law incident.

**Oklahoma Supreme Court — public reprimand of Matthew Reeves (former Butler Snow partner) — May 2026 — multi-jurisdictional discipline pattern.** The Oklahoma Supreme Court publicly reprimanded attorney Matthew Reeves for personally using a generative AI tool to add citations to two motions filed on behalf of a client in litigation involving the Alabama Department of Corrections, without independently verifying the AI-generated citations before filing. The court's framing of the underlying duty: attorneys have an obligation to review and verify all AI-generated information before filing, and signing pleadings containing unverified AI-generated citations demonstrates "a reckless disregard for accuracy and truthfulness." Reeves was a partner at Butler Snow at the time of the underlying filings. The opinion is structurally significant on two dimensions for this repo's design framework. First, it is the third state-supreme-court order in 2026 (after Nebraska's April 15–16 *Lake* license suspension and Alabama's April 24 *Hall / Ibach* conditional filing-bar) to act directly against an attorney for AI-hallucination misconduct, confirming the state-direct-disciplinary path as a multi-jurisdiction pattern across three independent supreme courts in successive monitor cycles. Second, it is the first published 2026 disciplinary action to record a *multi-jurisdictional discipline cascade* against a single attorney: Reeves had previously been sanctioned or disciplined in Alabama, Texas, and Tennessee for the same underlying incident before the Oklahoma reprimand landed. The cascade pattern is structurally distinct from the repeat-offender escalation of failure mode #9 (which involves multiple separate filings drawing successive sanctions in the same jurisdiction) and from the disciplinary-tier ladder of *Lake* and *Hall / Ibach* (which culminate in a license-impacting consequence in a single jurisdiction): the multi-jurisdictional cascade applies when an attorney holds licenses in (or has appeared pro hac vice in) multiple jurisdictions, and a single AI-hallucination incident propagates discipline through each licensing-and-admission jurisdiction in turn. The operational implication for the repo: any matter where the responsible attorney holds multi-jurisdiction admissions should carry the Reeves pattern as a known reciprocal-discipline risk, and the firm's intake skill should capture the full admissions roster as part of the matter record so that the discipline-cascade exposure is visible at intake rather than discovered after the first sanction lands.

**Ninth Circuit — *LNU v. Blanche*, No. 24-4790 (9th Cir. June 3, 2026) — Judges Paez, Bea, and Forrest — first published court-of-appeals opinion establishing a controlling AI-disclosure-and-verification standard; first circuit-level prospective firm-wide AI-certification remedy.** Attorneys Mike Singh Sethi and William Rounds filed briefs in immigration petitions containing multiple nonexistent cases (including "*Eduardo v. Garland*, 28 F.4th 742 (9th Cir. 2022)" and "*Lay v. Holder*, 729 F.3d 962 (9th Cir. 2013)," neither of which ever existed), quotations misattributed to real opinions (*Kamalthas v. INS* and *Avendano-Hernandez v. Lynch*), and gross misrepresentations of real authority. When the court denied submission on the briefs, counsel filed a Motion to Correct recasting the two nonexistent cases as "typographical errors" and substituting real cases with similar names. At oral argument the court asked four times whether generative AI had been used; Rounds denied it repeatedly before conceding it was "possible," then revealed that the brief writer was an unlicensed individual and that no licensed attorney had read the cited cases. The court issued an Order to Show Cause, identified parallel defects in the reply brief and in other Sethi filings, and imposed discipline: a $2,500 sanction against each attorney; a six-month suspension from practice before the Ninth Circuit; an order to serve the opinion on every client, opposing counsel, and presiding judge in every pending state or federal matter and on every attorney at the firm; service of the order on the State Bar of California; and — the remedy that distinguishes the opinion — a *prospective firm-wide certification regime* requiring Sethi, Rounds, and **all attorneys at the firm** to include in every future filing a statement, under penalty of perjury, that (a) discloses whether generative AI was used, (b) names the tool used, and (c) certifies that the signing attorney personally reviewed the filing and that all citations and quotations refer to existing authority. *LNU v. Blanche* is significant on five independent dimensions for this repo's design framework, and it fires the longest-standing open next-scan-priority item in this entry — the first published court-of-appeals decision establishing a controlling AI-disclosure or AI-verification standard. First, it is the first *full reasoned* circuit opinion squarely articulating the AI-citation verification duty — distinct from the Fifth Circuit's *Fletcher* (a short sanction order treating the matter as a lawyering problem) and the Sixth Circuit's *Whiting* (which did not expressly find AI use) — and it states the governing line cleanly: "the rules are not violated at the point of research and drafting, but at the point of signing and filing," so "it generally does not matter if the hallucination came from an AI tool or from the attorney's own reasoning." Second, it adds a *read-and-reason* duty on top of existence-and-on-point checking: "a competent and diligent attorney must do more than prompt generative AI, check that the citations provided by the AI are real and the subject matter roughly on point, and call it a day. A competent and diligent attorney must also *read* and *reason*." The signer's signature is a personal attestation of substantive review that cannot be conditioned on the tools a subordinate used or on the signer's ignorance of that use — Sethi's admission that he and Rounds "do not normally vet citations used by the Brief writer" the court called "an extraordinary confession." Third, it introduces a *fabrications-vs-inaccuracies* taxonomy and holds that the subtler category is the more dangerous one: "fabrications" are cases or quotations that do not exist; "inaccuracies" cite real authority but for a proposition the authority does not support. The court warns that "inaccuracies may prove more dangerous to our profession in the long run" because they evade facial checks and require "close analysis of cited sources," and cites Magesh et al., *Hallucination-Free? Assessing the Reliability of Leading AI Legal Research Tools*, 22 J. Empirical Legal Stud. 216 (2025), for the finding that legal-specific tools from Westlaw and Lexis returned inaccurate answers to 17% and 33% of a representative 2024 query set respectively — and rejects the vendor practice of defining "hallucination" to include only fabrications as "plainly irrational." The court's enumerated inaccuracy error-modes (misunderstanding holdings, presenting a rejected party argument as the court's holding, and failing to respect the hierarchy of authorities) map directly onto the repo's Characterization-error (category C) and Statutory/rule-text (category D) buckets and supply circuit-level authority for treating those buckets as first-class verifier targets rather than secondary to fabrication checks. Fourth, it supplies a *circuit-level affirmation of the candor-mitigates framework* the District of Maine *Hyde School / Guagenty* order established at the trial-court level (failure mode #13): "If, in the Motion to Correct, Sethi and Rounds had disclosed that AI was used in the opening brief against firm policy and apologized for failing to check the brief, lesser sanctions may have been warranted. But that is not what they did. The gravity of discipline we impose, including the temporary suspension of practice, is owed to this repeated failure of candor." Fifth, it surfaces a *silent-substitution correction* failure-mode variant (failure mode #14 below): in a companion matter (*Contreras Pelayo v. Bondi*, No. 24-5168) Sethi filed a Notice of Errata swapping hallucinated citations for real ones without ever disclosing that the prior citations were hallucinations, which the court held insufficient — "Swapping a hallucination out for a real case does not correct the prior false claim that 'this is a real case'" — and treated as a continuing failure "to correct false statements of … law previously made to the tribunal" under Rule 3.3. The prospective firm-wide certification remedy sits at the top of the forward-looking-remedy ladder this entry tracks: where Alabama's *Hall / Ibach* conditional filing-bar required a co-signature on the sanctioned attorney's future filings in the issuing court, *LNU v. Blanche* binds **every attorney at the firm** to an affirmative, perjury-backed AI-disclosure-and-personal-review certification on **every** future filing before the Ninth Circuit — the first published 2026 order to convert the institutional second-verifier and AI-tool-log disciplines this repo operationalizes into a court-ordered, firm-wide, signature-attached condition of continued practice.

**Northern District of Mississippi — *Withers v. City of Aberdeen* — June 2026 — Judge Sharion Aycock — first 2026 *both-sides* (bilateral) sanction; supervising-counsel liability for pro-hac-vice co-counsel; pro-hac-vice-revocation remedy.** In a contract dispute over unpaid legal fees (the plaintiff, Tom Withers, was himself an attorney but appeared through counsel and was not sanctioned), the court entered a sanctions order finding that the filings of *both litigants* contained AI-fabricated authorities — what the court described as "an unusual scenario—attorneys for both litigants engaged in similar sanctionable conduct." Four attorneys were sanctioned: on the plaintiff side, Kathleen Wilson (admitted using an AI tool to draft a filing) and Shauncey Hunter Ridgeway; on the city's side, Kathryn Young Williams (admitted using an AI tool for research) and Mark McClinton. The court removed all four from the case, barred all four from appearing before the Northern District of Mississippi for two years, and imposed $8,000 in total monetary penalties. *Withers* is significant on three independent dimensions for this repo's design framework. First, it is the first published 2026 opinion to sanction *both sides of a single case* for AI-hallucination misconduct — a bilateral failure pattern structurally distinct from every prior anchor case (each of which sanctioned one side). The practical lesson for the repo's `pre-filing-independent-review` design is that the opposing-counsel-as-error-surfacing-mechanism the entry relies on (the *Prince Group* dynamic, where opposing counsel caught the filing firm's hallucinations) *fails when both sides are committing the same defect* — neither adversary's filings can serve as the independent check on the other's, and the court itself becomes the first and only verifier. A bilateral hallucination case removes the adversarial backstop and makes the firm-internal institutional checkpoint the only line of defense. Second, it is the first published 2026 opinion to hold a *supervising / local attorney* liable for the AI hallucinations of *pro hac vice co-counsel* whose filings the supervisor signed off on without independent verification. Ridgeway and McClinton testified that they did not personally use AI; the court found them "negligent and careless but not purposeful," credited their corrective efforts, but held the sanctions stand because each was responsible for the work of the out-of-state pro-hac-vice attorney operating under his supervision. This extends the Rule 5.1 / 5.3 supervisory-failure pattern (failure mode #6, anchored in *Gutierrez* and *Cassata*) to the specific configuration of *local counsel supervising admitted-pro-hac-vice co-counsel*: signing a co-counsel's filing without a substantive cite-and-quote check carries the same non-delegable verification duty as signing one's own associate's or paralegal's work, and "I did not use AI; my co-counsel did" is the same losing defense as "the paralegal did it." Third, it surfaces a *pro-hac-vice-revocation* forward-looking remedy: Williams and Wilson were practicing pro hac vice (licensed out of state, admitted for this matter), and the court indicated it may revoke that status — barring them from practicing in Mississippi going forward — a remedy that sits alongside the Alabama *Hall / Ibach* conditional filing-bar and the Ninth Circuit *LNU v. Blanche* firm-wide certification as a third species of forward-looking, practice-restricting consequence. The opinion also records a *cross-jurisdictional repeat* dimension: per the court, Williams had been sanctioned for similar AI-hallucination filings in March 2026 in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Western District of Louisiana, reinforcing the multi-jurisdictional-discipline-cascade pattern (the *Reeves* anchor) and the repeat-offender-escalation pattern (failure mode #9). The clean operational read for the repo: the `pre-filing-independent-review` skill's chain-of-custody audit must extend explicitly to co-counsel and pro-hac-vice-counsel work product (not only in-firm drafters), and the matter-intake record should capture the full co-counsel and pro-hac-vice roster so the supervising attorney's non-delegable verification duty over co-counsel filings is visible at intake rather than discovered at the sanctions hearing.

## What the Courts Are Punishing

The opinions share a common structural critique. The sanctionable conduct is not the use of AI tools. It is the combination of:

1. Reliance on AI-generated citations without opening the cited authority in a primary database.
2. Failure to use a conventional citator (Shepard's, KeyCite, BCite) on the AI output before filing.
3. Signing and filing under Rule 11 or its state equivalents when the signer had not personally verified the work.
4. Escalation from a single missed check to a systemic practice pattern — multiple briefs, multiple matters, same firm or same attorney.
5. (Emerging — post-April 2026 *Prince Group* incident.) Existence of a firm AI policy that was not followed on the specific filing, combined with the absence of an institutional second-verifier who would have caught the policy departure. The argument that "the firm has a policy" now works against the signer once the opposing side shows the policy was not enforced on this filing.
6. (Emerging — *Gutierrez v. Lorenzo* and *Cassata v. Michael Macrina*.) Failure of supervision under Rule 5.1 (lawyer supervision) and Rule 5.3 (nonlawyer assistance), independent of whether AI was actually involved. Firms that delegate substantive drafting to paralegals, junior associates, or AI tools without a substantive cite-and-quote check before signature are sanctioned under the same supervision-rule framework whether the source of the bad authorities was a chatbot or a paralegal. The "AI did it" defense and the "the paralegal did it" defense fail in the same way and for the same reason: a supervising attorney signed the filing.
7. (Emerging — *Couvrette / Brigandi* and *Gutierrez*.) Post-error cover-up. When the firm's response to a show-cause order is to file affidavits blaming a former associate, blaming the AI tool, or shifting attribution among colleagues without disclosing the underlying drafting and review record, the court treats the concealment as an aggravator on top of the underlying citation-fabrication or supervision-failure conduct. The clean response is prompt, candid disclosure of who drafted, who reviewed, what tools were used, and what the verification record was — even when that disclosure is unflattering.
8. (Emerging — Eastern District of Pennsylvania, April 27, 2026.) *AI used to verify AI.* The signer drafts with one generative tool and "checks" the output with a second generative tool, treating the second model's confirmation as the verification step. The court treats this as a categorical verification failure rather than a partial credit, because the second tool shares the generative pathology of the first — both can produce plausible citations to authorities that do not exist. The verification step must be performed against a primary legal database (Westlaw, Lexis, Bloomberg Law, Fastcase, the official reporter, or the issuing court's docket), not against a second AI chatbot. The same pattern would fail under FRCP 11 and the candor rule whether the underlying authority were drawn from a chatbot, a paralegal's draft, or a junior associate's research, because the verification did not put a human eye on the primary source.
9. (Emerging — Eastern District of Pennsylvania, April 27, 2026.) *Repeat-offender escalation.* Courts faced with a second filing from the same attorney containing AI-fabricated authorities are not treating the second filing as a repeat of the first. They are stepping up the response on a multi-rung ladder — monetary sanction (first offense) → enhanced monetary sanction plus mandatory continuing-legal-education hours specifically on AI-and-legal-ethics, with proof-of-prior-CLE filing requirements (second offense) → bar-referral threat conditioned on a third offense (warning issued at the second). The escalation ladder is now an explicit feature of the published-opinion record and should be assumed by any firm whose lawyers have already drawn one AI-hallucination sanction.
11. (Emerging — Alabama Supreme Court, April 24, 2026.) *Apology-with-AI.* When counsel discovers AI-fabricated authorities in a prior filing and files a corrective response (an apology footnote in a reply brief, a notice of correction, an erratum, an apology letter to the tribunal, or a show-cause response), the corrective response itself is treated as a fresh filing for which the signer carries the same Rule 11 / RPC 3.3 verification duty as the underlying brief. If the corrective response is drafted with the same generative tool that produced the defect — and if the cite-and-quote check on the corrective response is delegated to that tool — the result observed in *Hall / Ibach* is that the apology recommits the same fabrication failure inside the document that purports to remediate it. The court treats the second-order failure as an independent verification-and-candor violation under Rule 3.3 and as conclusive evidence that the signer's representations about future compliance ("the mistake will not recur") cannot be credited. The apology-with-AI pattern is structurally distinct from the post-error cover-up of failure mode #7 (which shifts attribution to evade responsibility) and from the denial-of-AI-on-direct-inquiry of failure mode #10 (which misrepresents AI provenance to the tribunal). The clean response: any apology, erratum, notice of correction, or show-cause response in a matter where AI-hallucinated authorities have been identified is drafted by hand or by a second human attorney who did not draft the original filing, is run through the same verification sweep as the underlying brief (preferably under a stricter standard, since the corrective response is the document that signals to the court whether the firm has internalized the lesson), and is signed only after the AI-tool log entry for the corrective response itself has been recorded. The verification-by-second-AI-tool pattern condemned in failure mode #8 applies *a fortiori* to corrective responses: a corrective response cannot be verified by the same generative tool that produced the underlying defect.

12. (Emerging — Alabama Supreme Court, April 24, 2026.) *Cross-jurisdictional citation jumbling.* A defect-pattern variant in which an AI tool substitutes a citation but leaves the quoted proposition attached to the wrong jurisdiction, the wrong matter type, or a withdrawn or vacated opinion. *Hall / Ibach* surfaces two clean instances: a purported Nebraska Court of Appeals citation that traced to an unrelated Iowa criminal matter, and a quotation attributed to an Illinois trust opinion that traced to a withdrawn Indiana ruling. The pattern matters operationally because it can survive a citation-existence check (the court name and reporter format the AI returns parse as plausible against the cited jurisdiction) and a quotation-verbatim check (the quoted text exists in the form attributed) — the failure surfaces only when the verifier cross-checks that the *cited authority* and the *quoted proposition* line up against the same opinion in the same jurisdiction at the same procedural posture. The verifier should treat any AI-generated citation that pairs a quotation with an opinion in a different jurisdiction or different matter type than the surrounding argument as a YELLOW-or-RED-by-default item, and the citation cross-validation prompt should explicitly ask the model to confirm the cited authority's jurisdiction, matter type, and current status (in force / vacated / withdrawn / superseded).

13. (Emerging — District of Maine, May 5, 2026 — mitigating-side counterpart.) *Candor-and-remediation mitigates.* The federal-district sanctions record now contains an explicit *mitigating-side* counter-anchor to failure modes #7 (post-error cover-up), #10 (denial-of-AI-on-direct-inquiry), and #11 (apology-with-AI). In *Hyde School / Guagenty*, the underlying citation-defect pattern in the opposition brief — two improperly cited federal cases and an inaccurately quoted state-statute provision attributed to AI-assisted drafting — would have supported a Rule 11 monetary sanction on the docket precedent. The court declined to impose a monetary penalty, ordered AI-and-ethics CLE and new firm-level guardrails as the operative remedy, and identified counsel's voluntary disclosure of AI provenance (before any direct judicial inquiry), acceptance of responsibility, and expressed remorse as the basis for the non-monetary outcome. The mitigating-side framework that *Hyde School / Guagenty* establishes is structurally symmetric to the aggravating-side framework: the same disclosure pattern the court reviews to apply a candor-failure multiplier is the pattern it reviews to remit a monetary penalty, and the determinative variable in both directions is the timing and completeness of the disclosure relative to judicial inquiry. The operational implication for the repo's pre-filing checkpoint, certification-checklist, and AI-tool log design is that the log is dual-purpose — it is both the defensive record against a *Lake*-style denial-on-direct-inquiry contradiction and the predicate the signing attorney needs on the day a defect surfaces to make prompt-and-complete disclosure the available choice. The mitigating-side reading is conservative: the *Hyde School / Guagenty* remedy is not an entitlement created by candor; it is a discretionary remedy the trial judge selected on the record before her, and the candor pattern is necessary but not sufficient (the underlying defect count and severity remain inputs to the remedy selection). A firm whose pre-filing process delivers prompt disclosure when defects are discovered is positioning for the mitigating-side remedy; a firm whose process delays or evades disclosure is positioning for the *Lake*, *Hall / Ibach*, or *Couvrette / Brigandi* aggravators. The *Hyde School / Guagenty* mitigating-side framework does not weaken any failure-mode entry above; it adds the symmetric upside the failure-mode entries did not previously articulate.

10. (Emerging — Nebraska Supreme Court, April 15–16, 2026.) *Denial-of-AI-on-direct-inquiry.* When a tribunal questions counsel directly — at oral argument, on a show-cause hearing, or in a status conference — about whether AI tools were used in preparing a filing whose citations are visibly defective, an initial denial of AI provenance that is later contradicted by the attorney's own show-cause response is treated as an independent duty-of-candor violation under Rule 3.3 (and its state equivalents), separately sanctionable from the underlying citation-fabrication conduct. *Lake* establishes that a single first-instance disciplinary action that combines a heavy citation-defect count with a denial-on-direct-inquiry can support a license-suspension order without any prior monetary-sanction record. The clean response is the same one that defeats failure mode #7 (post-error cover-up) at the trial-court sanction level: prompt, candid disclosure of which AI tool was used, at which stage of drafting, and what the verification record was — even when that disclosure is unflattering, and especially when the inquiry is direct.

14. (Emerging — Ninth Circuit, June 3, 2026.) *Silent-substitution correction.* When counsel discovers a hallucinated citation in a filed brief and files a corrective notice (errata, notice of correction, motion to correct) that simply swaps the fabricated authority for a real one without disclosing that the prior citation was a hallucination, the correction is insufficient and the original false statement persists. *LNU v. Blanche* holds that by citing a hallucination and signing the brief, the attorney "previously attested to the accuracy, *and thus the existence of*," the fabricated authority; "[s]wapping a hallucination out for a real case does not correct the prior false claim that 'this is a real case'," and the omission is an ongoing failure "to correct false statements of … law previously made to the tribunal" under Rule 3.3. The silent-substitution pattern is structurally distinct from the post-error cover-up of failure mode #7 (which shifts attribution to evade responsibility), the denial-of-AI-on-direct-inquiry of failure mode #10 (which misrepresents AI provenance), and the apology-with-AI of failure mode #11 (which recommits the fabrication inside the corrective document): silent substitution makes a *facially clean* correction that conceals the defect's nature. The clean response is the inverse of failure mode #11's prescription: a corrective filing must affirmatively state that the prior citation did not exist (or did not support the proposition), identify the defect as a hallucination where that is the cause, and — per the *Hyde School / Guagenty* and *LNU v. Blanche* candor-mitigates framework — disclose the AI provenance and the verification failure rather than presenting the swap as a routine cite fix. A correction that reads as a typographical-error fix when the underlying defect was a fabrication is itself a candor violation.

The "I trusted the tool" defense has not landed. The "associate did it" defense has not landed. The "the paralegal did it" defense has not landed (*Gutierrez*). The "it was cite-checking software that hallucinated" defense has not landed. The "we have a written AI policy" defense has not landed — see *In re Prince Global Holdings Ltd.* (firm's own apology letter acknowledges policies not followed). The "we'll explain it later" cover-up has not landed (*Couvrette / Brigandi*; *Gutierrez*). The "I had a different AI tool verify the citations" defense has not landed (E.D. Pa., April 27, 2026 — Judge Scott's second-strike opinion expressly characterizes this as a verification failure rather than a partial verification). The "the citation was just a typographical error" reframe has not landed where the underlying defect was a fabrication, and the silent swap of a hallucinated case for a real one — without disclosing the prior citation never existed — has been held to leave the original false statement uncorrected (*LNU v. Blanche*, 9th Cir., June 3, 2026). The "I only reviewed the brief; a subordinate drafted it and I did not know AI was used" defense has not landed: the signature is a personal attestation of substantive review, and "checking that the citations provided by the AI are real and the subject matter roughly on point" is not enough — the signer must *read and reason* (*LNU v. Blanche*). The "I did not use AI" denial-on-direct-inquiry defense has not landed and has independently *added* a duty-of-candor violation on top of the underlying citation-fabrication conduct (*In re Discipline of Lake*, Neb. Sup. Ct., April 15–16, 2026 — initial denial of AI use at oral argument, later contradicted by the attorney's show-cause response, supported a license-suspension order). The consistent holding is that verification is a non-delegable duty of the signer, the verification must be performed against a primary legal database rather than a peer AI tool, supervision is a non-delegable duty of the supervising attorney and the firm, candor about which AI tool was used and at which stage of drafting is a non-delegable duty owed to the tribunal that survives any incentive to deny under direct judicial inquiry, and firms that put the institutional checkpoint on paper without putting it into workflow are increasingly at risk that the paper will be read against them.

## Expanded Defect Taxonomy (post-April 2026)

The *Prince Group* apology letter, the 6th Circuit *Whiting* opinion, and the California *Noland* opinion together push the repo-internal defect taxonomy past "bad citations" into four operational error categories that any drafter-side and reviewer-side sweep should address:

- **A. Citation errors.** Fabricated cases; wrong reporter, volume, year, or pin cite; non-existent subsections. The majority of 2025 sanctions were in this bucket.
- **B. Quotation errors.** Quoted text that does not appear verbatim in the source; ellipsis that hides qualifying language; quotation marks around paraphrase. The Oregon tariff schedule and the 5th Circuit *Fletcher* sanction weight these higher than citation errors.
- **C. Characterization errors.** The citation is real, but the draft says the court held X when it held Y — or treats dicta as holding, a concurrence as the majority, or a reversal as an affirmance. These dominate the *Prince Group* Schedule A catalogue and are the hardest errors for the signer to catch without reading the underlying opinion.
- **D. Statutory and rule-text errors.** Bankruptcy Code, U.S.C., state code, or FRCP/local-rule text quoted incorrectly or to a subsection that does not exist. Several of the *Prince Group* corrections fell in this category. The drafter-side citation verifier traditionally focuses on A and B; the reviewer-side sweep is the natural home for C and D.

The Ninth Circuit's June 3, 2026 *LNU v. Blanche* opinion supplies circuit-level authority for treating categories C and D as the *higher-priority* verifier targets rather than the residual ones. The opinion separates "fabrications" (categories A and B — authority or quoted text that does not exist) from "inaccuracies" (categories C and D — real authority cited for a proposition it does not support) and holds that "inaccuracies may prove more dangerous to our profession in the long run" because they survive facial checks and "often requires close analysis of cited sources" to detect. The opinion cites Magesh et al. (2025) for the finding that legal-specific tools from Westlaw and Lexis returned inaccurate answers to 17% and 33% of a representative 2024 query set respectively — i.e., that inaccuracies are common even in current-generation tools that fabricate less — and enumerates three recurring inaccuracy modes the sweep should specifically test for: misunderstanding a holding, presenting a rejected party argument as the court's holding, and failing to respect the hierarchy of authorities (e.g., citing a trial-court decision as binding on a circuit). A drafter-side or reviewer-side sweep that confirms only that a citation exists and that quoted text appears verbatim (categories A and B) has not discharged the verification duty the opinion describes; the proposition-to-holding alignment check (category C) and the statutory/rule-text accuracy check (category D) are where the more dangerous, harder-to-catch defects live.

## Governing Authorities to Cite Internally

When a firm's AI policy, engagement letter, or skill-output certification references the sanctions landscape, the following authorities carry the weight:

- Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(b) — signature as certification of legal and factual support.
- ABA Model Rule 3.3 (candor toward the tribunal) and jurisdictional equivalents (e.g., Oregon RPC 3.3, New York Rule 3.3, California Rule 3.3).
- ABA Model Rule 1.1 cmt. 8 (duty of competence, including technology competence).
- ABA Model Rule 5.1(a)–(c) (responsibilities of partners, managers, and supervisory lawyers) and Rule 5.3(a)–(c) (responsibilities regarding nonlawyer assistance, which courts have read in 2025–2026 to extend to AI tools used by lawyers and by nonlawyers under the firm's supervision). Both *Gutierrez v. Lorenzo* and *Cassata v. Michael Macrina* are anchored here.
- Any local standing order on AI disclosure — a growing number of district and appellate courts now require an affirmative statement when AI was used in preparing a filing.
- State bar ethics opinions issued 2023–2026 on generative AI use in practice. The California State Bar's COPRAC, at its March 13, 2026 meeting, approved proposed amendments to RPC 1.1, 1.4, 1.6, 3.3, 5.1, and 5.3 for a 45-day public comment period that closed on May 4, 2026, 11:59 p.m.; the proposal remains under COPRAC review for an anticipated submission to the California Supreme Court (which holds ultimate rule-amendment authority). The proposed amendments make the AI applicability of each of these rules express rather than implied. At its May 14–15, 2026 meeting, the Board of Trustees separately approved COPRAC's publication of the updated 2026 Practical Guidance for the Use of Generative Artificial Intelligence in the Practice of Law (the "2026 Practical Guidance"), which replaces the 2023 version. At the California Supreme Court's request, the 2026 Practical Guidance addresses agentic AI specifically and reiterates that a lawyer must attain baseline technological competence — a reasonable understanding of the relevant AI system's capabilities, data sources, limitations, and material risks and benefits — before deploying it in legal services, and that any AI use must not diminish or abdicate professional judgment, with the lawyer fully responsible for any outputs and work product generated with AI assistance. The Practical Guidance is advisory rather than disciplinary in form (it does not, by itself, carry the enforcement weight of the proposed RPC amendments), but its express coverage of agentic AI is the first state-bar guidance document to scope the duty-of-competence analysis to multi-step agent workflows rather than single-prompt assistance, and supplies the standard-of-conduct baseline a California court could read into RPC 1.1 even before the proposed RPC amendments are finalized.

## Operational Implications for This Repo

### For every drafting skill

Any skill in this repo that produces text that will be filed, served, or sent externally (`demand-letter-drafter`, `discovery-response-drafter`, `legal-research-memo`, `privilege-log-reviewer`, `regulatory-compliance-checker`, `deposition-prep-outline`, `contract-clause-analyzer`) must assume that every citation and every quotation in its output is unverified until a human confirms it. Output requirements should steer the user toward the `ai-citation-verifier` sweep before filing.

### For the citation verifier

The `ai-citation-verifier` skill is the canonical drafter-side pre-filing sweep. Its GREEN / YELLOW / RED labeling, verification queue, cross-validation prompts, and Rule 11 / RPC 3.3 certification checklist map directly to the failure modes the 2026 opinions describe. Any drafting skill that touches citations should cross-reference it.

### For the pre-filing independent review

The `pre-filing-independent-review` skill is the institutional counterpart to the citation verifier and the design response to the April 2026 *Prince Group* incident. The independent reviewer is an attorney who did not draft the filing, audits the chain-of-custody of the draft (which AI tools touched it, at which stage, under whose account), runs the five-category error sweep across the full draft (citations, quotations, characterizations, statutory/rule text, and operative details and deliverable completeness — dates, amounts, party names, record citations, severity labels, and required filing elements, swept at 100%), checks adverse authority, and either issues a Release-to-File attestation or returns a defect list. Tier 3 filings (appellate briefs, emergency motions, sanctions responses, any filing against a sophisticated adversary, and any filing where a generative AI tool touched the draft) should not ship without this pass. Post-*Gutierrez*, the chain-of-custody audit also asks who actually drafted the underlying language and whether the supervising attorney has substantively cite-checked the work product — the audit is agnostic as to whether a human paralegal, a junior associate, or an AI tool produced the language; the supervisory rules apply equally either way.

### For AI governance

`ai-governance-legal.md` covers the standing governance frame (attorney accountability, confidentiality, competence, transparency). This entry supplies the 2026 enforcement overlay. Firms setting their AI policy should:

- Require a documented verification pass, not just attorney review, before any AI-assisted filing.
- Treat ungrounded AI output (no retrieval over a primary legal database) as categorically higher risk, and raise the default verification burden accordingly.
- Log every AI-assisted filing in a way that would survive a judicial inquiry — model used, prompts, retrieval source if any, verifier, and verification timestamp.
- Train on the pattern recognition of AI-fabricated citations: unusually clean quoted language, plausible case names paired with implausible reporters or years, sweeping rule statements with no hedges.

### For intake and matter opening

Consider adding a line to the engagement letter template and the `client-intake-summary` skill flagging that AI tools may be used in matter work, the firm verifies AI-generated authority against primary sources before filing, and the firm logs AI-assisted work product per policy. This is both a transparency measure and a defensive record if a later filing is challenged.

The rule's clean form: verification must be performed against a primary legal database, not against a second generative AI tool, and a signer who relies on the latter is treated as having performed no verification at all.
- Opposing-counsel strategy: motions framed around an opponent's AI-generated filings, coupled with requests for fee-shifting under Rule 11 or the inherent authority of the court. *Prince Group* makes opposing-counsel review a live litigation weapon, not only a correction mechanism.
- Malpractice carrier underwriting terms conditioning coverage on documented AI verification workflows and documented institutional second-verifier processes. By April 2026, several legal professional liability carriers have begun appending AI-specific renewal questionnaires asking whether the firm has a written AI Use Policy, whether AI vendors are inventoried and their security practices documented, whether AI-assisted filings are logged, and whether the firm runs a documented pre-filing independent review on appellate and emergency-motion work. Firms with no documented controls are facing exclusionary endorsements; firms with documented controls are seeing renewal terms hold steady. The repo's `pre-filing-independent-review` and `ai-citation-verifier` outputs are intentionally written in a form that doubles as carrier-facing documentation.
- Adoption by additional state bars of California-style proposed RPC amendments making the AI applicability of competence (1.1), client communication (1.4), confidentiality (1.6), candor (3.3), and supervision (5.1 / 5.3) express. The California COPRAC March 13, 2026 amendments are the leading template; their 45-day public comment period closed May 4, 2026, 11:59 p.m., and the proposal is now before COPRAC for review before advancing to the Board of Trustees in May 2026 — the close of the comment period is the trigger for evaluating which state will be the first to follow California in adopting express-AI rule amendments. The New York, Florida, Texas, and Illinois state bars are the most likely next adopters.
- Additional published opinions following the District of Maine *Hyde School / Guagenty* mitigating-side framework — where prompt voluntary disclosure of AI provenance and acceptance of responsibility produce a non-monetary remedy (CLE + firm-guardrails) on a defect pattern that on the federal-district sanctions docket would otherwise support a Rule 11 monetary award. The candidate jurisdictions are federal districts whose existing 2026 sanctions docket already includes a monetary order for a comparable defect count, where the next show-cause record contains a documented pre-inquiry voluntary disclosure; published opinions in this pattern would validate that the mitigating-side framework operates independently of the trial judge and is a defensible institutional design target rather than a single-judge outlier.

The Lavern 10-pass verification pipeline is the first published reference architecture to formalize a richer-than-four-category verification taxonomy on the orchestrator side; the next-cycle question is whether the repo's four-category error sweep (citations / quotations / characterizations / statutory text) in `ai-hallucination-sanctions-2026.md` should extend toward an analogous multi-pass taxonomy for `pre-filing-independent-review` and `ai-citation-verifier`, and whether the Lavern Precedent Board pattern is the right template for a future playbook-evolution mechanism in `contract-clause-analyzer`, `nda-triage`, and `legal-response-templates`. *(Partially resolved 2026-07-13: the July 7, 2026 independent Legal Agent Benchmark leaderboard — which grades legal deliverables against atomic pass/fail criteria and reports a >90% per-criterion pass rate against a <15% all-pass rate — supplied the empirical case for extending the sweep, and `pre-filing-independent-review` v1.2 added a fifth category covering the operative-detail atoms the original four omit: dates and deadlines, dollar amounts and computed totals, party and entity names, record citations, severity and risk labels, and required-deliverable-element completeness, swept at 100% rather than sampled. - A published opinion sanctioning a firm where the show-cause record reveals a *Gutierrez* fact pattern — supervisory failure dressed up as an AI-blame defense — and the court treats the AI cover story as an independent ground for sanction (e.g., separate from the underlying citation fabrication or the underlying paralegal-drafting issue). - Additional *both-sides (bilateral)* sanction opinions following the Northern District of Mississippi *Withers v.
